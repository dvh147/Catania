# Chapter 4: Day by Day — A Sample Week

---

> *This is a suggested itinerary, not a contract. Things will go sideways.
> The toddler will have opinions. It will rain on a day you planned for the
> beach. A restaurant will be closed for a cousin's wedding. Embrace all of
> it. The best travel moments are usually the unplanned ones — and Sicily
> is very good at producing them.*

---

A few structural notes before we get into the days:

**Toddler nap logic** is built into every day. In practice, this means
protecting the midday window (roughly 12:30–3pm) for something stationary —
a long lunch, a café, the hotel — so the child sleeps and the adults sit
still and drink wine without guilt. The days are not packed. There is
breathing room. This is intentional.

**The weather** in Catania in March is typically mild (16–20°C) with
occasional rain. Each day below has a rain version — or notes on why
a bit of rain doesn't actually matter for that particular day.

**The car** stays parked in the city. You walk everywhere in Catania.
On day trip days, the car leaves early and comes back by late afternoon.

**The format for each day:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Morning** | Best hours — cooler, energetic, less crowded |
| **Midday** | Nap window — strategic stillness |
| **Afternoon** | Second wind — often the best sightseeing slot |
| **Evening** | Passeggiata, aperitivo, dinner at Sicilian time |
| **Ratings** | Stroller friendliness / Budget / Exertion level |

---

## Day 1 — Arrive & Find Your Feet

*Mood: slow, orientation, arrival. Do not attempt anything ambitious.*

You have just driven from the airport with luggage and a toddler who has
been on a plane. Your ambitions for this day should be low, and your
enjoyment will be correspondingly high.

### Morning / Early Afternoon
Check in. Drop the bags. Park the car — you won't need it again until
the first day trip (see Chapter 3 for parking options near the historic
centre). Let the room happen for a few minutes.

Then: walk to **Piazza del Duomo**. This is your first proper moment in
Catania, so take it at toddler speed. Find the elephant fountain. Explain
the elephant. (See Chapter 2 for the full Liotru legend — the short version
for now: there was a sorcerer, there was an elephant, it is now a fountain,
and Catania loves it more than anything.) Let the child point at things.

Sit at one of the bars on the piazza. Order a **granita con brioche**. This
is important. You are not ordering a granita as a treat — you are ordering
it as a cultural orientation exercise. The almond or pistachio is
recommended for first-timers; the coffee is excellent if you need the
caffeine. Dip the brioche into the granita. Do not question it.

### Midday
Lunch somewhere close and easy — a trattoria or tavola calda within a few
minutes' walk of the piazza. This is not the day for adventurous eating.
Order pasta alla Norma, which is Catania's own dish and available everywhere,
and see whether the local version convinces you (it will).

Nap window. Back to the accommodation, or a café if the timing allows it
to happen in a pram.

### Afternoon
Once everyone is operational again: a gentle walk up **Via Etnea**. Not
to do anything in particular — just to see the street, get your bearings,
buy gelato, look at the volcano at the far end of the road. This is
reconnaissance. You're building the mental map.

The **Villa Bellini** public park is a 15-minute walk north of the
cathedral — if the toddler needs to run around and you need to sit on a
bench and do nothing, this is an excellent option. It has paths, trees,
and very little that requires any effort from you.

### Evening
Aperitivo at 6:30–7pm. Find a bar with outdoor tables if the weather
allows. Order a glass of local white wine or an Aperol spritz, something
small to eat. Watch the passeggiata happen around you. This is the start
of the evening, not a preamble to dinner.

Dinner at 8pm or after, within walking distance of where you're staying.
Keep it local, keep it simple, go to bed at a reasonable hour.

> **Tonight:** look up the weather forecast for the week. It will inform
> how you order the days that follow — specifically whether Etna (needs
> clear skies) and the coast day (needs at least no heavy rain) should
> move earlier or later in the week.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★★★★ Easy — flat piazzas, wide streets | € — One of the cheapest days | Low — this is not a day for doing things |

## Day 2 — Deep Catania

*Mood: city explorer. This is the day Catania earns its place in the guide.*

### Morning
**6:30am if you can manage it. Definitely before 9am.**

**La Pescheria**, the fish market. This is the reason to set an alarm.
The market is at its peak between 7am and 10am — by late morning it's
winding down and the magic is diluted. Go early, go without a plan,
and just move through it slowly.

A practical note: the market is down a short flight of steps from the
street (a ramp is usually available at the side). Inside it's narrow
and the ground can be wet. A carrier rather than the pushchair is
recommended for this stretch — 30 minutes, maximum, then back to the
pushchair for the rest of the morning.

Buy something to eat from one of the stalls at the edge of the market.
Boiled octopus, if you're feeling brave before 8am. Fried things in
paper cones. A roll with whatever the vendor decides you should have.
This is breakfast in a different mode.

From La Pescheria, walk north on **Via Etnea**: Collegiata facade (stop,
look up, notice the black and gold), **Via dei Crociferi** (duck into
this side street — it looks like a baroque film set and essentially is
one), and then the entrance of the **Benedictine Monastery** if you
haven't booked a tour for a rainy day. The facade alone is worth the walk.

### Midday
Lunch near **Piazza Bellini** — the square with the Greek-Roman theatre
visible from the street level. Several good restaurants and bars in this
area; the clientele is mostly university students, which means the prices
are honest. Order a secondo of fresh fish if the morning market put you
in the mood.

Nap window: back to the accommodation or a long café stop while the
pram does its work.

### Afternoon
Two options, depending on mood and how the morning went:

**Option A:** The **Roman Theatre** — down the steps, into the excavation,
the extraordinary experience of seeing ancient Rome built under a
neighbourhood. Twenty minutes is enough; longer if the adults are into
it. From there, the **Amphitheatre at Piazza Stesicoro** is a 10-minute
walk — a Roman ruin under a traffic roundabout, best seen from street
level and best appreciated by thinking hard about what's underneath the
whole city.

**Option B:** If the morning was full and the toddler has opinions about
visiting more things: **Castello Ursino** (15 minutes' walk south from
the cathedral). The exterior and courtyard are worth the walk. Museum
inside at your discretion — it's quiet and dark, which can be either
a positive or a negative depending on current nap status.

### Evening
**Passeggiata** on Via Etnea, drifting south toward the Duomo as the
light fades. This is the evening walk Catanesi do every day — join it
rather than fighting it. Stop for gelato. Observe the social theatre.
End up back at the Piazza del Duomo at golden hour, when the cathedral
and the elephant are at their most photogenic.

Dinner in the Borgo area if you're up for a 20-minute walk north of
the centre — this is where locals actually eat, and the restaurants are
less tourist-facing. Or stay near the centre if the toddler situation
recommends it.

> **Rainy version:** La Pescheria still works in rain (partially covered,
> actually better light). Skip the outdoor afternoon options and replace
> with the Benedictine Monastery tour (book ahead) or Castello Ursino
> museum. A very long lunch is not a failure — it is, in Sicily, the point.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★★☆☆ Mixed — carrier needed for market, side streets tricky | €€ — some entrance fees, lunch out | Medium — a lot of walking, but all flat |

## Day 3 — Mt Etna

*Mood: adventure, altitude, the thing everyone comes to Sicily to see.*

**Check the weather the night before.** Etna in cloud cover is not Etna.
If the forecast shows low cloud or rain at altitude, swap today with a
city day and push Etna to when conditions improve. A wasted cable car
ticket is a real thing.

### Morning (early start — aim to leave by 7:30am)
Drive south from Catania toward **Nicolosi**, then up to **Rifugio
Sapienza** — the cable car base station on the south side. Allow 45
minutes from central Catania to the car park at Rifugio Sapienza; the
road up the volcano is winding and slow in the final section.

**At Rifugio Sapienza:** the cable car runs from here to approximately
2,500m. It takes about 15 minutes each way. At the top, 4x4 jeep
transfers take you up to approximately 2,900m, closer to the summit
craters. The jeep ride is exhilarating — rough lava terrain, dramatic
crater views — and is, in the experience of most visitors, worth the
extra cost.

**Bring layers.** This cannot be said firmly enough. Catania in March
can be 18°C at sea level. At 2,900m, it can be 3°C with wind. The
temperature shift is dramatic and rapid. Pack a proper jacket for
every member of the family, including the toddler.

**The pushchair does not go above the cable car base station.** The
terrain above is raw volcanic rock and wind. A carrier is essential
for the toddler from the cable car upward; you can leave the pushchair
folded at the base station.

At the top: the smell of sulphur (distinctive — the toddler will have
thoughts about this), the texture of the lava rock (crumbly, black, still
warm in places near fumaroles), the scale of the crater landscape (on a
clear day you can see the full curve of the Sicilian coastline below), and
the extraordinary silence when the wind drops. This is unlike anywhere
else you will go on this trip.

### Midday
Back down by cable car by noon or just after — the mountain clouds up
in the afternoon, so an early descent is wise anyway. Lunch at one of
the restaurants near Rifugio Sapienza, or better: drive a few minutes
down to **Nicolosi** or **Zafferana Etnea**, where you'll find local
trattorias serving unpretentious food at local prices. Zafferana is
also the honey capital of Etna — worth stopping for a honey and pastry
if you're passing through.

Nap in the car on the drive back if the timing works.

### Afternoon
Back in Catania by 2–3pm. The afternoon is yours — keep it low-key after
the morning's exertion. A walk, a gelato, nothing that requires thought.

### Evening
Dinner with something to celebrate — you went up a volcano with a toddler.
This deserves a proper sit-down meal. Consider somewhere in the Borgo
area that does grilled fish or the Catanian mixed grill.

> **Rain / cloud version:** If conditions are bad, do not go. Swap today
> with the Benedictine Monastery + Castello Ursino + long lunch city day,
> and revisit the Etna plan when the forecast clears. It is worth waiting
> for a good day.

> **Budget note:** Cable car + jeep is the expensive option and worth it.
> Cable car only (cheaper) still gets you to 2,500m and the lava field views.
> Driving up and walking the lower lava fields (free) is a legitimate
> choice for families with a toddler who won't appreciate the upper craters —
> the 2001 and 2002 lava flows are visible from the road and still dramatic.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★☆☆☆ Pushchair stays at base; carrier essential above cable car | €€€ — cable car + jeep + lunch out | High — altitude, terrain, cold |

## Day 4 — The Coast

*Mood: relaxed, salty, restorative. The toddler's favourite day.*

### Morning
Drive north from the historic centre — 20 minutes to **Aci Trezza**.
Park near the port.

**Aci Trezza** is a small fishing village with one very distinctive
feature: the **Faraglioni** — a group of dramatic black basalt sea
stacks rising straight out of the turquoise water just offshore. These
are the rocks that the blinded Cyclops Polyphemus, according to Homer's
Odyssey, hurled at Odysseus's escaping ships. They have been sitting
here, looking implausibly cinematic, for several million years.

Walk along the waterfront. Look at the rocks. Consider briefly the fact
that you are standing in the geography of one of the oldest stories in
Western literature. Then get a coffee and let the child run at the sea.

From Aci Trezza, **Aci Castello** is either a short drive or a 20-minute
walk along the coast road. The Norman castle sits on a basalt platform
jutting into the sea at an angle that seems structurally improbable but
is clearly working fine, given that it has been there since the 11th
century. The exterior view is the main event; the small museum inside is
optional. Climb up if the toddler's energy level allows it.

### Midday
Lunch at a seafront trattoria in Aci Trezza or Aci Castello — the fish
is very fresh (you watched it come in this morning) and the pricing is
lower than in central Catania's tourist zone. Order the grilled swordfish
if it's on. Or the pasta with clams. Or both.

Nap window — in the pram along the seafront, or at a quiet café table
while the sea does its thing nearby.

### Afternoon
Beach time, if the weather is cooperating. The beaches in this stretch
are volcanic sand (dark grey, not the white sand of brochure dreams) with
rock platforms for paddling. Not ideal for building sandcastles, but
excellent for toddlers who want to investigate rockpools.

For proper sandy beach: **Lido di Plaia**, south of Catania (15 minutes
by car), is the main city beach — long, wide, sandy, with lido facilities
(sun loungers and umbrellas for hire). More commercial, easier with a
toddler. In March it may be quiet enough to be very pleasant.

Drive back to Catania by 5pm, before the early evening traffic builds.

### Evening
An easy evening — you've had a full day of sea air. Aperitivo near
the accommodation, dinner somewhere nearby. Sleep comes easily after
a coastal day.

> **Rain version:** Aci Castello in light rain is actually dramatic —
> the castle over a grey sea, the rocks disappearing into mist. Worth
> doing anyway if it's only light. Heavy rain: skip the coast entirely
> and drive to **Acireale** instead (10 minutes further north from
> Aci Trezza) — a beautiful baroque town with excellent pastry shops
> and a covered market, entirely manageable in the wet.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★★★☆ Good along the seafront; rock platforms need a carrier | € — Cheaper eating, free beach areas | Low — this is the rest day |

## Day 5 — Taormina

*Mood: spectacular. You will take approximately 400 photographs.*

### Morning (leave early — 7:30–8am)
Drive north on the A18 motorway: 50 minutes from Catania. The drive
along the coast is beautiful — the sea visible to your right, Etna behind
you, the hillside towns appearing and disappearing.

**Parking:** do not attempt to drive into Taormina. The town sits on a
cliff 250m above the sea and has correspondingly limited road access.
Park at one of the lower car parks — **Mazzarò** (near the beach at
the base of the cliff) is the most convenient. From there, the cable car
(**funivia**) takes you up to the edge of town in a few minutes. The
cable car runs frequently; the views going up are already impressive.

**At the top:** walk up toward the **Teatro Greco-Romano** and go first
thing. The theatre is one of the finest ancient sites in Sicily — Greek
in origin (3rd century BC), substantially rebuilt by the Romans, still
hosting performances in summer. But the thing that makes it truly
extraordinary is not the theatre itself: it's the view. The stage
looks directly out to sea, and on a clear day Etna sits in the background
as if the theatre were designed specifically around the volcano. Which,
aesthetically speaking, it more or less was.

Go early. The difference between 9am and 11am at the theatre in terms
of crowd density is significant — tour groups begin arriving mid-morning
and the atmosphere shifts. Early, you can stand in the upper tiers with
almost no one around you, which is when it becomes genuinely moving.

**Stroller note:** the approach to the theatre involves steps and uneven
stone. A carrier for the toddler makes the visit significantly easier;
the pushchair can be left at the entrance.

### Midday
Back into town along the **Corso Umberto** — the main pedestrian street,
largely tourist shops (ceramics, lemon products, linen) with some genuinely
good food stops among them. Lunch at a restaurant just off the Corso,
slightly back from the main drag where the prices and the quality both
improve.

**Piazza IX Aprile**, the main terrace viewpoint on the Corso — do not
miss this. It has a balcony over the coastline that is one of the genuinely
jaw-dropping views of the whole trip. Sit at the café here for one drink
at minimum.

Nap window: a café table on the Corso, or a bench in the **Villa
Comunale** (public gardens) — slightly eccentric English-designed gardens
with great sea views and enough space for a toddler to run. Good for:
sitting on a bench doing nothing in particular, which after a morning
of theatre and cliffs is exactly right.

### Afternoon
If energy allows: explore the back streets of Taormina away from the
Corso. The town is small enough that getting slightly lost is easily
corrected. There are good ceramics shops tucked into side alleys — the
Caltagirone style (vivid yellows and blues) makes excellent and packable
gifts.

Drive back by 4–5pm to beat the early evening traffic on the A18.

### Evening
A quieter evening — Taormina days tend to be full. An aperitivo near
home, dinner somewhere local and unpretentious. You'll have seen enough
grandeur for one day.

> **Rain version:** Taormina in light rain is genuinely good — the
> tourist numbers drop significantly and the town feels more like itself.
> The theatre in mist is beautiful in a different way. Bring an umbrella
> and go anyway. Heavy, driving rain: postpone and use this day for the
> Benedictine Monastery tour in Catania instead, pushing Taormina to a
> better day.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★☆☆☆ Carrier needed for theatre; Corso is manageable | €€€ — parking + funivia + lunch in a tourist town | Medium — a lot of walking on uneven ground |

## Day 6 — Syracuse & Ortigia

*Mood: the big one. The most culturally rich day of the week. Give it space.*

This is the day that requires the most from you — not in terms of physical
effort, but in terms of attention. Syracuse is not a backdrop. It is one
of the most historically significant cities in the ancient world, and
its old town is one of the most beautiful in Sicily. It rewards being
present.

### Morning (early start — leave by 8am)
Drive south on the A19 motorway: 1 hour from Catania, straightforward.
Park in the area around the **Neapolis Archaeological Park** — there
is parking at the site entrance.

**The Neapolis Park** (allow 2–3 hours, depending on pace):

The **Greek Theatre** first, while your energy is fresh. This is a proper
Greek theatre — 5th century BC, one of the best preserved in the world,
still used for performances of Greek drama in alternating years. It is
enormous in a way that photographs don't quite convey. Sit at the top of
the seating tiers and look down at the stage; look back up at the sky.
Allow a moment.

The **Roman Amphitheatre** is immediately adjacent. The contrast with
the Greek theatre next door is instructive: the Greeks built spaces for
drama and communal storytelling; the Romans built a space for spectacle
and combat. Both are extraordinary. One is more comfortable to contemplate
than the other.

The **Ear of Dionysius** — a massive artificial cave cut into the cliff,
shaped roughly like a human ear in cross-section, with acoustics so
remarkable that a whisper at one end is audible at the other. The name
was supposedly given by Caravaggio, who visited in the early 17th century.
The legend: the tyrant Dionysius used it as a prison and could eavesdrop
on his prisoners through the acoustic properties of the rock. Whether or
not this is true, the cave is extraordinary — cool, dim, and geometrically
perfect in a way that is difficult to explain without going inside it.

**Stroller note:** the park has gravel paths and some uneven ground.
The pushchair gets through but a carrier is useful for the cave section.
The path to the Greek theatre is manageable; the approach to the Roman
amphitheatre is slightly rougher.

### Midday
Cross to **Ortigia** — a 10-minute drive, or a 20-minute walk along the
waterfront. Ortigia is a small island connected to the mainland by two
short bridges; the entire historic old town is on the island.

Lunch in Ortigia. The streets just back from the main waterfront have
the best balance of quality and price — avoid the obvious tourist-facing
restaurants on Piazza Archimede and explore the lanes behind them.
**The Ortigia market** (Via Trento, morning only) may still have stalls
running if you arrive by noon: excellent for street food and local produce,
compact and manageable.

Nap window: Ortigia's waterfront has benches, and the toddler in a pram
can sleep to the sound of the sea while the adults sit and look at the
water. This is one of the better nap-window locations of the week.

### Afternoon
Walk Ortigia without a plan:

The **Cathedral** — built directly on top of a 5th-century BC Greek
temple, with the original Doric columns still visible incorporated into
the walls. This is the most extraordinary architectural layering in the
guide: you stand inside a baroque cathedral and see ancient Greece
holding it up. It is free to enter. Take the time.

The **Fountain of Arethusa** — a natural freshwater spring at the edge
of the island, inches from the sea. In Greek mythology, the nymph Arethusa
fled from the river god Alpheus underwater from Greece to Sicily, and
emerged here as a spring. The spring actually exists; the papyrus plants
growing around it (a plant associated with Egypt and the Nile, here in
southern Sicily) are real. The myth is, at minimum, a wonderful explanation
for something genuinely strange.

The **waterfront** (Lungomare) for aperitivo as the light changes — the
east-facing coast of Ortigia gets beautiful late afternoon light. Order
something local and watch the boats.

### Evening
Drive back to Catania (1 hour) by early evening. Dinner late, at home
base — after a day at this intensity, something simple and close is the
right call. A good trattoria near the accommodation, a glass of Nero
d'Avola, and a conversation about what you saw.

> **Rain version:** Ortigia in the rain is genuinely beautiful —
> the pale limestone streets, the baroque architecture, the cathedral.
> Come anyway. For the Neapolis park: light rain is fine; heavy rain
> makes the open-air sections less pleasant. If forecast is bad,
> replace the park with the **Paolo Orsi Archaeological Museum** —
> one of the finest archaeological museums in Europe, entirely indoors,
> immediately adjacent to the park entrance. It holds up for 2 hours
> easily and covers much of the same ground as the park itself.

| Stroller | Budget | Exertion |
|---|---|---|
| ★★★☆☆ Ortigia excellent; park gravel paths harder | €€ — entrance fees + lunch, but very manageable | Medium-high — a long day on your feet |

## Day 7 — Slow Last Day in Catania

*Mood: savouring. Today has no agenda. The agenda is the problem.*

### Morning
Sleep slightly later than you have all week. Have a very good breakfast —
either at a bar you've come to love during the week, or an extended hotel
breakfast if that's available. Order extra pastries.

If you haven't been to **La Pescheria** yet this week for the full early
experience: go. Otherwise: go back anyway. The market is different the
second time — you know what things are, you recognise some faces, you
feel slightly less like a tourist and slightly more like a regular. This
is the best state to be in on a last day.

**The Mercato di Via Plebiscito** for practical shopping: blood oranges
to take home (buy more than you think you need — they barely survive the
journey but the ones that do are extraordinary), local honey, dried herbs,
good olive oil if your luggage allows it. This is where you spend the
last of the local cash.

### Midday
**Benedictine Monastery tour**, if you haven't done it yet — this is
the insurance option for any day that got rained out. Book the tour
in advance (it fills up). Two hours, entirely indoors, one of the most
atmospheric spaces in the city. The rooftop terrace at the end has the
best view over Catania's roofscape.

If you've already done the monastery: **Palazzo Biscari**, the private
baroque palace with the famous oval ballroom. Tours run on specific days;
check the schedule. It is the one building in the city that you won't
have covered otherwise.

Long lunch. There is no rush. The flights home are tomorrow or the day
after. Today, lunch takes as long as it takes.

Nap. Everyone.

### Afternoon
Return to **Piazza del Duomo** one more time. The elephant fountain looks
different when you know the story. Via Etnea, one last walk north with
the volcano at the end. The Collegiata facade in afternoon light.

Buy the thing you've been walking past all week and telling yourself you'd
buy later. Later is now.

### Evening
**The farewell dinner.** Not the first trattoria you find, but somewhere
you've identified during the week as the right place for this occasion.
A proper sit-down, a full meal from antipasto to dolce, a bottle of
something good. Sicilian wine for the adults; blood orange juice for the
toddler who has now consumed approximately their body weight in granita
over seven days and shows no sign of tiring of it.

**Cannoli for dessert**, filled to order in front of you, the shell
shattering, the ricotta fresh. This is how the week ends.

Then: the passeggiata. One last drift along Via Etnea as the city does
its evening thing around you. The street lights on the black lava paving.
The sound of Catania at night. The volcano in the dark, a red glow at
the summit if you're lucky and the mountain is in a mood.

Go to bed late. You're in Sicily.
